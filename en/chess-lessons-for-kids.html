MY APPROACH

I Teach More Than Chess — I Teach How to Think

In chess, knowing the rules, openings or tactical patterns is not enough. In every position, a player has to make a decision: find several possible moves, compare them, evaluate the advantages and disadvantages, and only then choose what to play.

This is the thinking process I try to teach my students. I do not want a child to simply memorize: “This is the move you should play.” I want them to understand: “Why is this move better?”

I often explain difficult chess ideas through simple real-life examples. This helps children understand the logic of the position and the underlying principle instead of memorizing a rule mechanically.

My goal is for the student to gradually rely less on ready-made answers from the coach and become more confident in finding the right decisions independently.

INDIVIDUAL TRAINING PROGRAM

We Focus on What Each Student Actually Needs

Every child has different strengths and weaknesses, so I do not use the same fixed program for everyone.

Opening Repertoire

We build an opening repertoire around the student's playing style. If necessary, we can create it almost from scratch.

Tactics and Calculation

We learn to spot tactical opportunities, calculate variations more deeply and make decisions based on concrete analysis rather than guesswork.

Strategy and Middlegame

We study plans, typical positions, weaknesses and piece activity, and learn what to do once the opening is over.

Endgames

We study important endgame positions and learn how to convert an advantage or defend difficult positions correctly.

Game Analysis

We analyze the student's own games, identify recurring mistakes and turn them into specific training tasks.

Tournament Preparation

Before competitions, we work on form, opening preparation, specific opponents and psychological readiness for the game.

TOURNAMENT PREPARATION

I Support Students Beyond Regular Lessons

If a child competes regularly, tournament preparation becomes a separate and important part of our work.

We analyze games, prepare the opening repertoire, study possible opponents and identify which areas of the student's play need the most attention before the tournament begins.

During a tournament, I can help the student prepare for upcoming games, and after each round we can analyze the game and identify the specific reasons behind mistakes.

I want the student not only to react emotionally to a loss or enjoy a win, but to understand what actually happened in the game and what lessons can be taken into the next round.

01

We analyze the opponent's playing style and possible opening repertoire whenever enough information is available.

02

03

Psychological Preparation

We work on handling nerves, maintaining concentration, managing expectations and recovering after a difficult game.

04

Adjustments During the Tournament

We use the games already played to quickly identify what should be adjusted before the next rounds.

WORK BETWEEN LESSONS

Progress Continues After the Lesson

One lesson alone is not enough for a new topic to become firmly understood. That is why work between lessons is an important part of the training process.

I give students individual homework and always check it. This helps me understand how well the student has absorbed the material and what needs to be reviewed in the next lesson.

When needed, I create a separate Lichess study for the student, where we can keep positions, variations, opening material and exercises for independent work.

If a topic needs to be reviewed again, the Zoom lesson can be recorded so the student can return to the explanation later and go through the material once more.

REGULAR TRAINING

Why I Focus on Consistent Training

Chess cannot be learned in one or two lessons. New knowledge needs to be reinforced, applied in games and revisited through mistakes in order to gradually build a deeper understanding of chess.

That is why regular lessons are the main format of my work. As a rule, I recommend training at least once a week so the student does not lose what they have learned between lessons.

If the goal is significant progress, stronger tournament results and systematic preparation, the optimal schedule is usually two or more lessons per week.

For regular students, I reserve a specific time in my schedule. This means there is no need to look for an available time slot before every lesson, and training can become a consistent, structured process.

A trial lesson is available for new students. After the trial, we determine the most suitable lesson format, training frequency and further training plan.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

About the Lessons

Answers to common questions parents ask before starting lessons.

What age can children start learning chess?

I work with children from around age 5. For younger students, shorter 30-minute lessons are usually the best option so they can stay focused and interested throughout the lesson.

Can a complete beginner take lessons?

Yes. I work both with complete beginners who are just discovering chess and with children who already have tournament experience and a competitive background.

Are the lessons suitable for children who already play competitively?

Yes. For more experienced students, I build the program after analyzing their games, strengths and weaknesses. We can work on opening repertoire, calculation, strategy, endgames and tournament preparation.

How do online lessons work?

Lessons are mainly conducted through Zoom and the Lichess online chessboard. We can analyze games, solve exercises, study openings and work with materials prepared specifically for the student.

Do you give homework?

Yes. I select assignments based on the student's current needs and always review the completed work. When needed, I also create a separate Lichess study with materials for independent training.

Can you prepare my child for a specific tournament?

Yes. With tournament players, we can prepare an opening repertoire, analyze possible opponents, review games played during the event and work on psychological preparation.

How often should my child take lessons?

For regular training, I usually recommend at least one lesson per week. If the goal is serious progress, tournament competition or reaching a higher competitive level, two or more lessons per week are usually the better option.

Can we book individual lessons without a subscription?

Yes, individual one-off lessons are available, but their price is significantly higher than the price per lesson within a subscription plan.

For effective long-term improvement, however, I recommend regular training. One or two lessons are not enough to build a systematic training process: new material needs to be reinforced, applied in games, reviewed through mistakes and gradually developed further.

For regular students, I create an individual training plan and reserve a specific time in my schedule. That is why a subscription is not only more cost-effective but also the main format of my work with students.

Are lessons available in English?

Yes. I conduct individual chess lessons in both Russian and English.
